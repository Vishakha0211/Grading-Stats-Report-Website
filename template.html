What changed

The 2024 batch experienced significant grade inflation across several high-credit freshman courses (MA105, MA110, MS101, and CS101). Averages peaked across the board, driven heavily by the mathematics department, where MA105 increased by 1.56 points and MA110 climbed by nearly a full point compared to 2023.

In contrast, the 2025 batch saw a distinct downward shift. Averages dropped across all major core courses: MA105 fell by 1.31 points, MA110 reverted to its 2023 baseline, BB101 hit a three-year low, and both CS101 and MS101 registered notable declines.

Standard Deviation of Elective Grades
Higher SD = wider spread of grades within a department's electives ("high risk, high reward"); lower SD = grades cluster more tightly.

Why standard deviation?

Standard deviation measures how spread out students' grades are. If two courses share the same average grade, the one with higher SD has grades more widely dispersed, a "high risk, high reward" scenario with more top (10) and low (6) grades. A course with lower SD keeps grades concentrated around 7–8, a "safer route" with fewer extremes.

How it's calculated: we first find the weighted average grade for each course offering in a department, then measure the statistical spread of those course averages relative to the department's collective mean.

Notes

  • Scope: covers the 17 minor departments running in the upcoming 2026–27 academic year (16 home departments + Robotics), using data tracked from 2022 to 2025.
  • Course standardization: focuses exclusively on core M-tagged courses under the Division column to establish a consistent departmental baseline. Fluctuation-heavy minor electives are excluded, as they vary entirely by individual student choice.
  • Robotics overlap: Robotics courses are cross-listed from courses of home departments, e.g. EE309 (Microprocessors) counts identically for both Electrical and Robotics since it is M-tagged in both.
  • Data reliability: some departments have far more data than others. Entrepreneurship (37 courses), Systems & Control (29) and BSBE (21) have solid baselines, while MM (1), Earth Sciences (2), Energy (3) and Humanities (5) have very few data points, treat those averages as rough indicators, not final proof.
